## 1. Impact on Students and How They Can Use This Game

### What is Endless Worlds?

Endless Worlds is a 2D educational game built in Godot Engine. The player explores a procedurally generated island world — walking over grass, sand, dirt, and water — while solving AI-generated riddles on subjects like programming, mathematics, or any topic the player picks. Every time a player answers correctly, they earn points, level up, and a new riddle gets generated. The AI adjusts how hard the questions are based on how well the player is doing.

At its core, the game is trying to make learning feel like play. Instead of reading a textbook or watching a video, you wander around an open world, pick up hints, and engage with real knowledge in a fun way.

---

### Why Does This Matter for Students?

A major challenge in education — especially in technical fields like Computer Science and Information Science — is **engagement**. Students often find it hard to stay motivated when learning abstract topics like loops, recursion, or data structures. Research consistently shows that **gamification** — applying game-design elements to learning — can improve motivation, retention, and long-term outcomes.

Here's what makes Endless Worlds relevant:

**1. Active Learning Instead of Passive Consumption**

Most educational tools deliver content passively — you read or watch. Endless Worlds demands active participation. The player must *answer* to progress, not just scroll through material. This forces recall, which is one of the most effective ways to actually remember things (this is called the *testing effect* in cognitive science).

**2. Immediate Feedback**

When a student answers a question wrong in a textbook, they often don't know until exam time. In Endless Worlds, feedback is instant. Wrong answer? You know right away, and hints are available if you're stuck. This closes the learning loop quickly.

**3. Adaptive Difficulty (No One Gets Left Behind)**

The game's reinforcement learning engine quietly watches how the player is doing. If you're breezing through questions without using any hints, it decides you're "bored" and bumps up the difficulty. If you keep getting things wrong, it backs off and makes questions easier. This means the game naturally fits beginners and advanced students alike without manual configuration.

**4. Any Subject, Any Student**

Because riddles are generated by an AI using web-scraped content, the game is not locked to one subject. An ISE student can set the topic to "computer networks." A student studying for a biology exam can type in "cell biology." The topic input makes it universally useful, not just for coding students.

**5. Exploration Rewards Curiosity**

Hint pickups are scattered across the map. To find them, you have to explore. This mirrors the real experience of learning — answers rarely come to you; you have to go looking. The game rewards curious, exploratory behavior, which is exactly the kind of mindset good engineers and researchers need.

---

### Who Can Use This Game?

| Student Type | How It Helps |
|---|---|
| First-year engineering students | Makes programming fundamentals (loops, variables, functions) engaging and interactive |
| Students preparing for exams | Topic-based riddles act as a self-test tool — type in any subject and quiz yourself |
| Students struggling with motivation | Low-pressure game environment removes fear of failure; hints are available |
| Students studying CS/ISE | Direct relevance to programming concepts integrated into the core game loop |
| Curious learners of any subject | The topic selector opens the game to history, science, math, or anything else |

---

### Broader Educational Benefits

**Gamification and Motivation**

Studies in educational technology show that game-based learning can improve motivation by making the process of learning feel rewarding. Points, levels, and high scores tap into the same psychology that makes video games addictive — except here the "winning" involves actually learning something.

**Retention Through Repetition**

Each new level brings a new riddle. Players who keep playing naturally encounter related questions multiple times, reinforcing memory. The Learning Journal feature (where solved riddles are saved) doubles as a personal study guide generated by the player's own gameplay.

**Reducing Anxiety Around Difficult Topics**

Programming is notoriously anxiety-inducing for beginners. The playful environment of Endless Worlds — with its weather effects, day-night cycle, water splashes, and exploration — creates a relaxed context for encountering difficult ideas. It's harder to feel anxious about recursion when you're also watching rain fall over an island at night.

**Data-Driven Personalization (Future Scope)**

The game already collects data like hints used, time taken, and wrong attempts. This data can be used to recommend what topics to focus on, which is a step toward fully personalized learning — something that traditional education struggles to provide at scale.

---

### How Can Students Practically Use This?

1. **As a Daily Quiz Tool** — Open the game, type in today's topic (e.g., "sorting algorithms"), and play for 15–20 minutes. The AI will generate fresh questions every session.

2. **As Exam Preparation** — A few days before an exam, cycle through relevant topics. The hint system prevents complete blockage, and the fact-reference shown after each riddle gives quick conceptual summaries.

3. **As a Group Activity** — Students can take turns answering riddles in a classroom setting. The score system makes it naturally competitive and fun.

4. **As a Self-Assessment Tool** — Check the stats panel on the home screen to see hints used, accuracy, and level. This gives a quick sense of how well you know a topic without formal testing.

## 2. Reinforcement Learning in Endless Worlds

### What is Reinforcement Learning?

Reinforcement Learning (RL) is a type of machine learning where an agent learns by **trial and error**. It takes actions, receives feedback (a reward or a penalty), and gradually learns which actions are best in which situations.

Think of it like training a dog. You ask the dog to sit. If it sits, you give it a treat (reward). If it doesn't sit, no treat (penalty). Over many repetitions, the dog learns that "sit = treat." RL works the same way, except the "dog" is a software algorithm.

The three key components are:
- **State** — the current situation (e.g., is the player finding things too easy or too hard?)
- **Action** — what the agent can do (e.g., make questions harder, easier, or keep the same)
- **Reward** — feedback that tells the agent if its action was good or bad

---

### What Algorithm is Used in This Game?

Endless Worlds uses **Q-Learning**, one of the most classic and well-understood RL algorithms. The code lives in `ai/DifficultyRL.gd`.

Q-Learning builds a table called a **Q-table**. Each row is a state, each column is an action, and each cell holds a number (the Q-value) representing "how good is it to take this action in this state?"

The update formula used in the game is:

```
Q[state][action] = Q[state][action] + α × (reward + γ × max_future_Q - Q[state][action])
```

Where:
- `α` (alpha) = **learning rate** — how quickly the agent updates its beliefs (set to `0.3`)
- `γ` (gamma) = **discount factor** — how much future rewards matter (set to `0.5`)
- `reward` = feedback from the current outcome
- `max_future_Q` = the best Q-value available from the next state

---

### States — How the Game Reads the Player

After every riddle attempt, the game looks at two things:
1. Did the player **win** (answer correctly)?
2. How many **hints** did the player use?

From these two facts, it determines a state:

| Win? | Hints Used | State | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yes | 0 | `BORING` | Player got it right without any help — it was too easy |
| Yes | 1 or more | `CHALLENGED` | Player got it right but needed help — this is the sweet spot |
| No | Any | `TOO_HARD` | Player couldn't answer — it was too difficult |

The game wants to keep the player in the `CHALLENGED` state as much as possible. That's the zone where learning actually happens — you're pushing yourself but not giving up.

---

### Actions — What the RL Agent Can Do

The agent has five difficulty levels:

```
VERY_EASY → EASY → MEDIUM → HARD → VERY_HARD
```

And three possible actions:
- **UP** — increase difficulty by one step
- **DOWN** — decrease difficulty by one step
- **SAME** — keep difficulty unchanged

---

### How It Plays Out — A Concrete Example

Let's say you're playing and you answer three riddles in a row correctly, without using a single hint.

**After riddle 1:**
- State: `BORING` (won, no hints)
- Q-table says: best action for `BORING` is `UP`
- New difficulty: goes from `MEDIUM` → `HARD`

**After riddle 2:**
- You used 2 hints to get this one right
- State: `CHALLENGED` (won, hints used)
- Q-table says: best action for `CHALLENGED` is `SAME`
- Difficulty stays at `HARD`

**After riddle 3:**
- You got it wrong
- State: `TOO_HARD` (lost)
- Q-table says: best action is `DOWN`
- New difficulty: `HARD` → `MEDIUM`

The system is continuously nudging the difficulty so the player stays challenged but not overwhelmed. This is called the **Flow State** in game design psychology — the ideal zone between boredom and anxiety.

---

### Initial Q-Table Values

The Q-table is pre-seeded with logical starting values so the agent doesn't have to learn from scratch:

```
BORING:    UP=2.0,   SAME=-1.0, DOWN=-2.0   (clearly too easy → go harder)
TOO_HARD:  DOWN=2.0, SAME=-1.0, UP=-2.0     (clearly too hard → go easier)
CHALLENGED: SAME=2.0, UP=0.5,  DOWN=0.5    (sweet spot → maintain it)
```

These values give the agent a head start. It doesn't need hundreds of rounds to figure out the obvious — if someone can't answer a question, don't make it harder.

---

### Persistence — The Model Remembers You

After every session, the Q-table and current difficulty are saved to a local file (`difficulty_rl.json`). When you restart the game, the agent picks up exactly where it left off. Over time, as you play more sessions, the agent's Q-table gets refined and its decisions become more precise for your specific learning patterns.

---

### Why Q-Learning is the Right Choice Here

Endless Worlds is a lightweight game meant to run in a browser and on mobile. A heavy deep-learning approach (like Deep Q-Networks with neural networks) would be overkill and slow. Q-Learning is:
- Fast to compute
- Small memory footprint (just a dictionary)
- Easy to persist (saved as a JSON file)
- Interpretable — you can look at the Q-table and understand exactly why the agent made a decision

This makes it a perfect fit for an embedded, real-time educational game.

## 3. Agentic AI — Current Implementation and Future Upgrades

### What is Agentic AI?

The word *agentic* means "able to act independently." In AI, an **agentic system** is one that can **plan**, **take multiple steps**, **use tools**, and **make decisions** to complete a goal — without a human directing every step.

This is different from a simple AI that just answers a single question. An agentic AI is more like a small team of specialists working together automatically:
1. One specialist **figures out what to search for**
2. Another **searches the web**
3. Another **reads the page**
4. Another **writes the question based on what was found**

Endless Worlds has exactly this kind of pipeline built into it, all triggered the moment you start a new level.

---

### The Current Agentic Pipeline

When a new riddle is needed, the game runs through a multi-step, automated process. Here's exactly what happens, step by step:

```
Player starts level
        ↓
Step 1: Topic Resolver (LLM)
        "Programming" → picks subtopic → "Recursion in Python"
        ↓
Step 2: Web Search (Serper.dev API)
        Searches Google for "Recursion in Python quiz questions and answers"
        Gets a list of web URLs
        ↓
Step 3: Web Scraper
        Picks first non-PDF result
        Downloads and cleans the HTML
        Extracts up to 5000 characters of readable text
        ↓
Step 4: Riddle Generator (Groq LLM / GPT)
        Uses the scraped text as context
        Generates a riddle, 4 options, answer, 4 hints, and a fact reference
        ↓
Step 5: Difficulty Filter (RL Agent)
        Adjusts the difficulty level passed to the LLM prompt
        ↓
Riddle shown to player
```

Each step uses the output of the previous step. This chain of operations — where an AI reasons, searches, reads, and generates — is the definition of an agentic AI system.

---

### Breaking Down Each Agent Role

**Agent 1 — The Topic Resolver**

The player types "Programming" into the topic box. That's too broad to generate a good, specific question. The first LLM call takes this broad topic and generates 5 related subtopics, then randomly picks one:

```
Input: "Programming"
Output: { "general_topic": "Programming", "chosen_subtopic": "Recursion in Python" }
```

This gives the subsequent steps something precise to work with. Without this step, every session on "Programming" would generate the same generic questions.

**Agent 2 — The Web Searcher**

Using the Serper.dev API (a Google Search API), the agent searches:

```
"Recursion in Python quiz questions and answers"
```

It gets back a list of organic search results with URLs, titles, and snippets. It picks the first non-PDF, non-document URL.

**Agent 3 — The Web Scraper**

The agent fetches the chosen URL's full HTML page, strips out all the JavaScript, CSS, navigation bars, footers, and HTML tags, and extracts clean readable text. This text is trimmed to 5000 characters to stay within the LLM's context limits.

**Agent 4 — The Riddle Crafter**

Now armed with real, up-to-date content from the web, the LLM generates a riddle in strict JSON format:

```json
{
  "riddle": "I call myself until a base case stops me. What am I?",
  "options": ["Recursion", "Loop", "Iterator", "Callback"],
  "solution": "Recursion",
  "hints": ["I appear in trees", "I call myself", "I need a base case", "Stack overflow risk"],
  "fact_reference": "Recursion is a technique where a function calls itself to solve smaller subproblems.",
  "source": "web"
}
```

The difficulty level (from the RL agent) is embedded in the prompt, so the LLM knows whether to write an easy or a hard question.

**Fallback Behavior**

If any step fails — no API key, web page doesn't load, LLM returns malformed JSON — the system has a fallback: a set of 6 pre-written programming riddles. The game never crashes or shows a blank screen. It degrades gracefully.

---

### What Makes This "Agentic"

Three properties define an agentic AI system:

1. **Goal-directed** — The overall goal is "generate a relevant, quality riddle for this player right now." The pipeline works toward that goal automatically.

2. **Tool use** — The AI uses external tools (a search API, a web scraper) to gather information it doesn't have internally.

3. **Multi-step reasoning** — The system doesn't answer in one shot. It plans and executes a sequence of steps, each dependent on the last.

Endless Worlds hits all three. This is not just an LLM answering a prompt. It's an LLM system that goes out into the world, finds information, and uses it — autonomously.

---

### How Agentic AI Can Be Upgraded in the Future

The current system is a solid foundation. Here's how it could be significantly expanded:

---

**1. Persistent Memory Agent**

Right now, the game doesn't remember which topics a specific player has already covered across sessions. A memory agent could maintain a personal knowledge profile:

- Topics already mastered
- Common mistakes made
- Concepts where hints were frequently used

With this, the riddle generator would avoid repeating questions the player already knows and focus on weak areas.

---

**2. Adaptive Curriculum Agent**

Instead of letting the player manually type a topic, an agent could analyze the player's entire history and *suggest* what to study next:

> "You've done well on loops and variables. Based on your progress, I recommend Functions next."

This moves from reactive personalization (adjusting based on what just happened) to proactive personalization (planning a full learning path).

---

**3. Multi-Agent Debate for Better Questions**

Currently, one LLM generates the riddle. A more robust approach would use multiple agents that critique each other:

- **Drafter Agent** — writes the riddle
- **Reviewer Agent** — checks if the answer is unambiguous, if all four options are reasonable, if the difficulty matches the requested level
- **Editor Agent** — rewrites based on reviewer feedback

This would dramatically improve question quality and catch errors the single-agent approach misses.

---

**4. Vision Agent for Non-Text Subjects**

For subjects like circuit diagrams, math proofs, or biology diagrams, a vision-capable agent could generate questions based on *images*, not just text. The player might be shown a diagram and asked to identify a component.

---

**5. Real-Time Emotional State Detection**

By tracking behavioral signals (long pauses, rapid wrong answers, frequent hint use), an agent could infer the player's emotional state — frustration, boredom, confusion — and adapt not just difficulty but the *style* of questions, the tone of hints, or whether to offer a break.

---

**6. Fully Autonomous Learning Path Generator**

The most ambitious upgrade would be a high-level planning agent that works like a teacher. The player sets a goal: "I want to understand how websites work in one week." The agent:

1. Breaks this into subtopics (HTML, HTTP, DNS, servers, JavaScript, etc.)
2. Schedules them across sessions
3. Monitors progress
4. Adjusts the schedule if the player falls behind or surges ahead
5. Generates a final summary report

This would turn Endless Worlds from a quiz game into a full personalized tutoring system.

---

### Summary of Agentic AI Levels

| Level | What It Does | Status in Endless Worlds |
|---|---|---|
| Single LLM call | Answers one question | ✅ Basic fallback exists |
| Tool-using agent | Searches web, scrapes pages | ✅ Fully implemented |
| Multi-step pipeline | Chains topic → search → scrape → generate | ✅ Fully implemented |
| Memory agent | Remembers player history across sessions | 🔲 Planned (data collection exists) |
| Planning agent | Designs full learning curriculum | 🔲 Future upgrade |
| Multi-agent system | Agents review each other's work | 🔲 Future upgrade |
